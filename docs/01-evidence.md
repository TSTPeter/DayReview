# Learning science: what the build rests on

Effect sizes are Cohen's d or Hedges' g unless stated. Links go to the source, not a summary.

## Tier 1: strong enough to design around

**Explicit spelling instruction beats incidental exposure.**
53 studies, 6,037 pupils, K-12. Against no instruction ES 0.54, against incidental 0.43,
maintenance 0.53, transfer into free writing 0.94, knock-on to reading 0.44.
[Graham & Santangelo 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-014-9517-0)

**Retrieval practice beats restudy, for spelling, in this age group.**
[Retrieval practice benefits for spelling in fifth-grade children, Memory 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09658211.2023.2248420) ·
[Cover-Copy-Compare meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/pits.20622)

**Spacing.** 254 studies, 14,811 participants: spaced beat massed by ~10.6 percentage
points (47.3% vs 36.7%). Optimal gap scales with the retention interval, roughly 10-20%
of it. There is no universal best gap.
[Cepeda et al. 2006](https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf) ·
[Cepeda et al. 2008](https://laplab.ucsd.edu/articles/Cepeda%20et%20al%202008_psychsci.pdf)

**High-information feedback.** 435 studies, ~61,000 participants. Overall d = 0.48, but
task/process/self-regulation feedback d = 0.99, corrective d = 0.46, and bare
reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24.
[Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)

Timing was not resolvable in that synthesis. Shute's review is the better guide: immediate
feedback suits difficult procedural tasks and lower-attaining learners, which is this case.
Shute also advises sparing praise and no normative comparison.
[Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)

**Morphological instruction.** 22 studies. Benefits spelling, reading and vocabulary,
**larger for less able readers**, and more effective combined with other literacy work than
taught alone.
[Bowers, Kirby & Deacon 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654309359353)

**Component evidence for struggling spellers.** 59 studies, 2,229 children, K-9, with or
at risk of learning disabilities. Whole-word study g = 0.56, multilinguistic g = 0.43,
phonemic approaches weaker for spelling but best for transfer to word reading (g = 0.45).
[Chandler et al. 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222194251364836)

"Multilinguistic" means phonology, orthography and morphology worked on the same word. That
is the closest published support for this app's core method.

## Tier 2: real, with caveats we must respect

**Interleaving spelling rules.** Preregistered, 147 German Year 3 children, four rules
including consonant doubling. Interleaved beat blocked on trained words immediately and at
eight weeks. Effects 0.18 to 0.42. Crucially, **no transfer to untrained words**, and
instructional guidance helped transfer only for children with high prior knowledge.
[Cognitive Research 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12511507/)

Design consequence: interleave the practice, but never assume the rule generalises. Teach
the rule explicitly and practise it across many words.

**Orthographic mapping.** Words become instantly retrievable when graphemes bond to phonemes
plus meaning, not by visual memorisation of word shapes.
[Ehri 2014](https://library.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/257/2019/07/ehri.pdf)

## Tier 3: the learner-profile finding that sets the target

267 children aged 8-9 across 143 Northern Ireland primary schools, all with identified
literacy difficulties, all with roughly four years of synthetic phonics behind them:

- ~84% of writing samples leaned heavily on phoneme-to-grapheme guessing
- under 14% of correct spellings demonstrated orthographic knowledge, meaning the right
  choice among several legal spellings of one sound
- they did **not** acquire orthographic patterns from reading exposure alone

[Frontiers in Education 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1641126/full)

This is the product thesis in one paragraph. The missing layer is orthographic choice, and
it has to be taught, not caught.

## Tier 4: weaker than it is usually presented

**Etymology as an instructional lever.** Strong theoretical case, almost no controlled
evidence that teaching word origin improves spelling accuracy. The EEF concedes limited
evidence on explicit vocabulary teaching generally.
[EEF on word roots](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-blog-getting-to-the-root-of-vocabulary-instruction)

Use origin as explanation and hook, shown after the attempt. Never as the retrieval mechanism.

**Structured Word Inquiry, the packaged form of the morphology-plus-etymology approach.**
Year-long RCT with Grade 3 and 5 poor readers and spellers found minimal differences against
a robust vocabulary plus reciprocal teaching comparison. Teaching assistants found SWI hard
to deliver, which the authors flag as a likely cause. A separate study found the same.
[Colenbrander et al. 2022](https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rrq.399) ·
[Georgiou et al. 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9817.12325)

The honest reading: the ingredients have evidence, the branded package did not beat a good
alternative, and delivery difficulty was a major factor. An app removes the delivery
variance, which is an argument for building it, not a guarantee it works.

## Error-based and adaptive instruction: read in full, September 2026

### Error-based exercises work, modestly, against a weak control

Rello et al. built 5,000 exercises from corpora of real dyslexic errors (2,654 English,
1,171 Spanish) and tested them as an iPad game, DysEggxia, against Word Search.
[Rello, Bayarri, Otal & Pielot 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/2661334.2661373)

Design: 48 children recruited, **43 analysed**, ages 6-11 (mean 8.79). Eight-week
within-subject crossover, 12 sessions of 20 minutes per phase, counterbalanced.

| Measure | DysEggxia | Word Search | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Words with errors | -5.0 pp | -2.3 pp | 0.355, **not significant** |
| Errors per word | -0.072 | +0.030 | **0.029**, r = 0.29 |
| Errors per incorrect word | -0.171 | +0.164 | **0.011**, r = 0.35 |
| Reading errors per word | -0.011 | -0.015 | 0.410, not significant |
| Subjective writing skill | +0.33 | +0.26 | 0.426, not significant |

Read that table before quoting the abstract. **The most intuitive measure, the proportion of
words containing an error, did not reach significance.** The two significant results are
error-density measures, both small to medium (r = 0.29 and 0.35). Reading did not improve, and
neither did the children's own sense of their ability.

The authors state the limits themselves: Spanish only, a transparent orthography, and no
untreated control arm.

**One design choice we should not copy.** DysEggxia deliberately shows the child a misspelled
word to correct, on the rationale that dyslexic readers cannot consciously detect their own
errors. That is defensible as error-detection training. It is the opposite of our rule, which
is never to display a wrong spelling as the object of attention. If we ever adopt it, adopt it
as a separate, labelled exercise type, never inside the main retrieval loop.

### Adaptivity did not beat a fixed sequence

The decision-relevant paper, read in full.
[Solheim, Frijters, Lundetræ & Uppstad 2018, *Learning and Instruction*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475217304565)

Thirteen Norwegian schools randomised, 744 Grade 1 children, 140 identified at risk. Three
arms: adaptive app (GraphoGame), fixed app (On Track ABC), and an enhanced business-as-usual
control. Twenty-five weeks, 100 sessions.

Both interventions beat control on word reading, sentence reading and spelling, all p < .001.
**Neither beat the other.**

| Outcome | Adapt vs Fixed | SE | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Word reading | -1.30 | 0.17 | 0.227 |
| Sentence reading | -1.69 | 1.09 | 0.121 |
| Spelling | +0.47 | 1.10 | 0.670 |

Effect sizes against control: **Adapt 0.38 to 0.64, Fixed 0.58 to 0.75.** The paper notes the
fixed condition's effect sizes were "notably larger", though the direct contrast was never
significant. On an extended spelling measure designed to break ceiling effects, Adapt g = 0.81
and Fixed g = 0.68, again untested against each other.

**Four caveats before over-reading it:**

1. The computer component was **10 minutes inside a 40-minute teacher-led session**. Adaptivity
   had a quarter of the dose in which to show an effect.
2. The adaptive feature was **difficulty adaptation** (GraphoGame targets an 80% success rate),
   not method adaptation. It never diagnosed an error type or changed instructional approach.
3. The two apps differed on more than adaptivity, despite the paper's framing. Their own
   Table 3 shows the fixed app contained **spelling** content and timed reading, immediate
   feedback and a reward system belonged only to the adaptive app.
4. Grade 1, Norwegian, semi-transparent orthography.

**What it changes here.** Adaptive difficulty selection is not established as better than a
well-designed fixed sequence, in the one trial that isolated it. Our claim is different, and
still untested: adaptive *method* selection driven by a diagnosed error type. But the prior
just got less favourable, so the fixed sequence becomes the default and adaptivity becomes the
experiment. See `docs/08-build-plan.md`.

### The per-child adaptive engine exists, and has no efficacy data

[Siddiqui et al. 2026, SPIRE, IUI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3742413.3789137)

Architecture: hypothesis filter, three parallel LLM trace generators, a trace selector, a
program synthesiser over a domain-specific language of speech-language-pathology instructional
moves, then an interface generator. Six move types: correct grapheme, identify graphemes,
phoneme table, definition, word matrix, suffixing convention.

**Evaluation is 10 experts rating 50 simulated transcripts, plus 7 children in usability
sessions. No learning outcomes were measured at all.** Treat it as architecture, not evidence.

Two findings from it that matter to us anyway:

- **Experts agreed on the reasoning and disagreed on the sequence.** Pedagogical reasoning
  validity scored 4.90 of 5 with 74.7% perfect agreement. Instructional action
  appropriateness scored 4.32 with only **26.7% perfect inter-rater agreement**, and the
  disagreement centred on ordering, such as whether to address morphology before
  grapheme-phoneme correspondence. So our escalation ladder is not merely untested. There is
  no expert consensus ordering available to copy.
- **Cost.** Eight LLM calls and roughly **$0.70 per single word inquiry**. For 103 words across
  a class, live generation is untenable. This confirms the offline-generation decision in
  `docs/05-architecture.md`.

## Modality: handwriting versus typing, read in full

Handwriting wins clearly for **acquiring new symbols**: novel alphabets in adults
([Wiley & Rapp 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797621993111)), novel
orthographies in kindergarten
([Ibaibarriaga, Acha & Perea 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-025-10703-2)),
with a proposed visual-motor mechanism
([James 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0963721417709821)).

For our population the picture is more specific, and more awkward.
[Broc, Goudeau & Colliot 2025, *Scientific Reports*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03369-x),
305 students, Grades 4 to 7, copying and narrative tasks in both media:

| Finding | Study 1 | Replication |
|---|---|---|
| Words copied | paper 42.09 vs keyboard 20.09, eta-sq 0.58 | paper 40.06 vs keyboard 22.22, eta-sq 0.49 |
| Spelling accuracy | no effect, p = .70, 98% both | **paper 0.95 vs keyboard 0.89**, eta-sq 0.13 |
| Errors per word | paper 0.01 vs keyboard 0.04, eta-sq 0.07 | paper 0.06 vs keyboard 0.11, eta-sq 0.05 |
| Medium x error type | significant, eta-sq 0.07 | significant, eta-sq 0.07 |

**The interaction is the finding that matters.** In the replication the keyboard penalty was
carried specifically by **orthographic** errors: 0.01 per word on paper against 0.06 on
keyboard, t(166) = -10.42. Phonological errors moved far less.

Note an inconsistency in the paper. The abstract states no significant differences in error
types between conditions. The results section reports a significant medium-by-error-type
interaction in both studies. Trust the tables.

**Why this is the most consequential result of the four papers.** This product exists to fix
orthographic choice errors, and typing appears to inflate exactly that error class. A typed
diagnostic will therefore overstate orthographic weakness. The narrative task showed no
accuracy difference, and this was copying rather than dictation, so do not overstate it. But
for **measurement**, paper is now the defensible medium. See `docs/03-diagnostic.md` and
`docs/07-assessment.md`.

Typing proficiency also moderates learning in the typed condition, with no equivalent effect
for handwriting
([Ouellette & Tims 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00117/full)),
and children with impaired fine motor skills learned decoding best by typing
([Suggate et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022096523000504)).
Neither obtained in full; both point the same way, which is to measure keyboard fluency.

## Refuted: do not build on these

**Learning styles / modality matching.** The meshing hypothesis, that instruction should
match a learner's visual, auditory or kinaesthetic preference, requires a crossover
interaction to be demonstrated. Pashler and colleagues found almost no studies using an
adequate design, and those that did found evidence contradicting the hypothesis.
[Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)

This kills "she is a visual learner, so use pictures". It does **not** kill switching method
after a failure, provided the switch is triggered by the error, not by a learner label.
See `docs/03-diagnostic.md` for the defensible version.

**Special dyslexia fonts.** 15 studies, 91 effect sizes, N = 688: g = -0.04, CI
[-0.15, 0.07]. No advantage over Arial or Times New Roman.
[Azzarello et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42536336/)

**Coloured overlays and tinted backgrounds.** 51 publications, 2,690 participants. Better
designed studies showed weaker effects; the authors conclude use cannot be endorsed.
[Griffiths et al. 2016](https://www.readwritecenter.org/uploads/1/1/2/8/112870549/colored_overlays-systematic_review_of_literature.pdf)

Generic legibility does help: adequate size, generous letter and line spacing, left
alignment, short lines. Offer font and colour as comfort preferences, never as a claim.
