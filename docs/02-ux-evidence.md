# UX and motivation: what the evidence permits

The brief is "fun, engaging and memorable". The evidence is unusually clear about which of
those three you buy with decoration and which you buy with design.

## Three popular ideas the evidence kills

| Idea | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Streaks and badges improve learning | Not established. Cognitive g = 0.49 is confounded, and motivational and behavioural effects went non-significant under rigorous designs |
| Dyslexia fonts help | Refuted, g = -0.04, N = 688 |
| Coloured overlays help | Refuted, 2,690 participants, effects shrink as rigour rises |

Gamification meta-analysis: [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w).
The authors note behavioural outcomes were "almost exclusively process data" collected
during the intervention, so gamification can inflate items attempted without inflating
learning.

## Rewards: the finding that should change the design

Expected tangible rewards reduce free-choice persistence. Engagement-contingent d = -0.40,
completion-contingent d = -0.36, performance-contingent d = -0.28. **Unexpected rewards
d = 0.01, no harm.** Verbal positive feedback helped overall (d = 0.33) but for children
specifically d = 0.11, non-significant. Rewards were more damaging for children than adults.
[Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)

A daily streak is precisely an engagement-contingent reward: the worst class, in the most
vulnerable group. It is also the feature closest to the ICO Children's Code prohibition on
exploiting "human affirmation needs".
[ICO on nudge techniques](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)

**So: no streak. Use unexpected rewards and self-referenced progress.** "You have got the
doubling rule three times running" beats "day 12". Show her the rules she has cracked, not
the days she has served.

## Where the fun actually comes from

Three sources, all of which survive the evidence:

1. **The reveal.** The word breaks apart and shows why it is spelled that way. Novelty and
   surprise, delivered after the retrieval attempt so it does not scaffold it away.
2. **Being right about something hard.** Self-referenced mastery, which is the motivational
   mechanism that does not backfire.
3. **The story.** "Yacht is a Dutch hunting ship" is memorable because it is odd and true.
   The etymology carries the delight even though it does not carry the memory.

## Multimedia and cognitive load

Mayer's programme, 181 studies, 591 effects, overall g = 0.37, with effect sizes declining
per year, which is the signature of early-study inflation.
[Meta-analysis 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X25000673)

Two principles change this build:

**Coherence and seductive detail.** The largest reliable effect. Decorative animation,
background music, celebratory motion during an item, all of it is the *removal* condition in
these studies. Cut them from the practice screen. Put them between items, where they cost
nothing.

**Redundancy, and its reversal.** 21 children with dyslexia and 21 typical readers: combined
audio plus written text hindered efficiency for both groups. Dyslexic children learned best
from audio plus pictures, typical readers from written text plus pictures.
[Knoop-van Campen et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/dys.1585)

Single study, n = 42, so hold it lightly. The design response is cheap: make audio a mode,
not a permanent overlay on displayed text. Never play the word aloud while the word is on
screen.

## Audio and text to speech

22 studies, d = 0.35 on reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities. Voice
type (synthetic, human, recorded) was not a significant moderator.
[Wood et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)

Synthetic voice is adequate. Do not pay for human recording on quality grounds. Do check the
pronunciation of every word on the list, because British English TTS gets "controversy" and
"privilege" wrong often enough to matter.

## Session length

No credible primary evidence for an optimal session length. The "ten minutes a day" figure
common in edtech marketing has no meta-analytic basis. Set it by observation, not by claim.

Sustained-use evidence is weak and confounded by the novelty effect: gamified engagement
declines after initial exposure.
[Novelty effect](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcal.12385)

## The EEF's position on edtech, which is the right frame

"Technology itself is unlikely to improve young people's learning." Gains come from
technology that improves the **quality of practice** or gives **precise feedback on
misconceptions**.
[EEF digital technology](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/digital)

That is exactly the two things this app does. It is also the sentence to put in front of any
teacher or head who asks why this is different from the last six spelling apps.

## Screen inventory, for the Claude Design canvas

Draft these six, no more, before any code:

1. **Attempt.** Hear the word in a sentence, type it. Nothing else on screen.
2. **Reveal.** Attempt marked against target, error named, morphemes, one line of why.
3. **Rule card.** The pattern, three sibling words, one worked example.
4. **Session end.** Self-referenced progress: rules cracked, words moved up, nothing about days.
5. **Diagnostic.** The 20-word baseline probe, styled as a low-stakes challenge, not a test.
6. **Grown-up view.** Error profile, pattern strengths, what to practise on paper.

Design constraints for all six: high contrast, generous line and letter spacing, left
aligned, short lines, tap targets over 44px, and no motion on screen 1.
